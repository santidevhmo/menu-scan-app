Menu Scan · evaluation

How we test whether the macros are right

Written for Santiago and for any session starting cold. It explains the whole measurement setup in plain language, defines every piece of jargon, and answers one blunt question: is the app actually getting better?

Section 01

What the app does, and what we are testing

A user photographs a restaurant menu. The app returns every dish with its calories, protein, carbs and fat, sorted by how well it fits their nutrition goal.

Three model calls do this. Only the last one is what we have been testing.

The one mechanical fact worth knowing. The model never adds anything up. It lists a dish's ingredients, and for each one says what that food is made of per 100 g and how much of it is on the plate. Our code does the multiplication and the sum. Every time arithmetic was taken away from the model, accuracy improved.

Section 02

Why there are two separate test sets

Some menus print a weight — NEW YORK (400gr). Most do not. These are two completely different problems, so they are scored separately and the two scores are never added together.

The benchmark is thin here, and that is a real weakness. Six dishes stand in for two-thirds of every real menu. One dish swinging can move the whole score by several points, which is why widening this set is the current priority.

Section 03

How one test dish gets built

This is the process Santiago defined at the start, unchanged. It is slow and manual on purpose — every number traces to a published source or a recorded decision.

  1. Pick a real dish off a real menu

    From the photographed menus, not invented. The dish's printed text is kept exactly as it appears.

  2. Write down what is actually in it

    Each ingredient and how many grams of it. For a dish with no printed weight, a plausible range rather than one number — a pizza is 400–450 g, not exactly 425 g.

  3. Look up each ingredient in USDA FoodData Central

    A public government nutrition database. Every ingredient gets a real record ID, so anyone can check it. The specific variant matters enormously — restaurant pizza and frozen pizza differ by 46% in fat.

  4. Santiago approves it personally

    No dish enters the test set without his ruling, and every later change to one is recorded with the reason.

  5. The right answer is computed, never typed

    Code multiplies the grams by the composition and sums. This keeps calories consistent with the protein, carbs and fat rather than trusting anyone's arithmetic.

Section 04

How we decide a value is right

The app's answer never has to match exactly. It has to land close enough. "Close enough" is a range around the true value, and each of the four numbers is judged on its own.

Where the range comes from — and what just changed

Until 20 August the range was a side-effect. It was calculated purely from how uncertain the dish's weight was, which meant every dish got a different strictness, and nobody had chosen any of them.

DishOld strictnessNowEffect
Capricciosa pizza±6%±20%looser
Salmón Roll±14%±20%looser
Tiras de pollo±17%±20%looser
Carbonara±29%±20%stricter

The pizza had to hit its fat within ±6% — a bar no real kitchen would meet twice. Carbonara got ±29% and passed almost everything. The test was partly measuring how tightly each dish happened to be written down. Now every dish is judged the same way.

The check that stops this being cheating. Loosening a test until the score looks good is rigging. So both the current app and the older, worse version are scored at every candidate bar. If the gap between them shrinks, the bar has stopped telling good from bad and is rejected.

Two bars were considered. ±25% would have moved the headline from 36 to 48 — Santiago rejected it for exactly that reason and chose ±20%, which leaves the score unchanged at 36/72 and the gap unchanged at 11. Only the meaning changed, not the number.

A percentage alone is unfair to small dishes

A percentage scales with the dish, which is why it was chosen. But that cuts both ways: on a small dish, 20% is a punishingly small number of grams.

DishTrue fat±20% allowsIn real terms
Capricciosa pizza42 g±8.4 ga generous margin
Ensalada griega12.5 g±2.5 ghalf a teaspoon of oil
Coliflor roka16.5 g±3.3 gunder a teaspoon

So a field now passes on either test — within 20%, or within a fixed small miss of 6 g for a macro and 50 kcal for calories. Whichever is more generous wins.

This rule is not new. Santiago made exactly this ruling for the printed-weight dishes on 9 August — "if something has 20 grams and the model says 15, that's only five grams, it's not that different" — and the no-weight scorer simply never got it. The two halves of the benchmark now agree on what "close enough" means, and share one copy of the numbers.

The honest cost, and it needs watching. The headline moves 36 → 44 and the gap over the old version narrows from 11 to 9. The gap surviving is the test passing its own anti-rigging check — but it did narrow, and a narrowing gap is the early sign of a bar that has gone soft. Worth re-checking as dishes are added.

What it does not do is rescue a genuinely wrong answer. Coliflor roka stays at 3/12: its fat is 14.5 g out, far past the 6 g allowance. A test pins this, so the allowance cannot quietly grow into the whole rule.

Section 05

Are we going in circles?

Fair question, and the honest answer is split: the app has improved a lot; today's work did not change the app at all.

Both directions are real gains, measured across multiple runs. Nothing has gone backwards.

One caveat on that last row, and it matters. The 44 and the 25 above are not measured the same way — the 25 predates both the ±20% bar and the gram allowance. Scored under today's rules the older version reads 35, so the honest gain is 35 → 44, not 25 → 44. Whenever the ruler changes, only same-ruler comparisons mean anything.

What today actually was

No change to the app. Three plausible fixes were proposed and killed before any money was spent on them:

IdeaResult when simulatedVerdict
Correct the side-dish weights46 → 66 wrongworse
Add fat to thin-looking dishes46 → 80 wrongworse
Correct each dish's total weight36 → 35 rightno gain

Each was tested by replaying every answer the model has ever given, with the proposed fix applied. Cost: nothing. All three looked obviously correct beforehand.

Why the corrections kept coming. Twice, a measuring script disagreed with the real one — reading 33 where the harness read 36. Both times it was caught by a deliberate rule: every simulation must first reproduce the known score exactly, or none of its other numbers are believed. That rule is doing its job, and it is why the corrections surface as noise. Silent agreement would be the dangerous outcome.

Open decisions

Approved · 2026-08-20 · not yet done

Replace Coliflor Roka in the test set

Its menu line is just the name. The real dish — seen on the restaurant's Instagram — is battered cauliflower on lettuce under a chipotle mayo. None of that is knowable from the menu, so the dish is unanswerable rather than badly answered, and it has been distorting the score. It will be swapped for something plainly described, such as a side of french fries.

Its replacement needs a new USDA-sourced recipe and Santiago's approval, the same route as any test dish.

Approved · 2026-08-20 · not yet done

Widen the no-printed-weight test set

Six dishes is too thin a basis for judging two-thirds of every menu. One dish swinging can move the score several points. More dishes means more USDA sourcing and more of Santiago's rulings, but no model spend.

Done · 2026-08-20

Allow a small miss in grams, not only in percent

Implemented as described in section 04, reusing the exact allowances Santiago ruled for the printed-weight dishes on 9 August rather than inventing new ones.

A stricter variant was considered and rejected: capping the error in grams, so a large dish could not drift far while still passing on percentage. Measured, only the carb cap would ever bind — a pizza at ±20% is allowed ±22.6 g of carbs, above the proposed 15–20 g cap. It would have made one macro on one dish harder and changed nothing else, so it was not worth a second rule.

Section 07

Every piece of jargon, defined

oracle
The recorded truth for a test dish — its ingredients, their grams, their USDA records, and the resulting correct macros. Santiago approves every one.
band
The range of answers counted as correct for one macro on one dish.
draw
One run of the model. The same dish is drawn three times because the model answers slightly differently each call, and a single run proves nothing.
weighted dish
A dish whose menu prints a weight. Nothing to do with statistical weighting.
unweighted dish
A dish whose menu prints no weight — two-thirds of real items.
arm
One candidate change being tested, so it can be compared against the current version. Named A, P, S3 and so on purely for record-keeping.
baseline
The older, worse version, kept as a comparison. If a change cannot beat it, the change is not real.
the gap
How far ahead the current version scores versus that baseline. It is the anti-cheating check: a test that cannot separate them is measuring nothing.
harness
The script that runs the test and produces the score.
replay
Re-scoring answers the model already gave, saved on disk. Costs nothing and calls no model, which is how ideas get killed for free.
the two scores
n / 288 counts values wrong on printed-weight dishes — lower is better. n / 72 counts values right on the rest — higher is better. They are never combined.
the allowance
The fixed small miss — 6 g of a macro, 50 kcal — that passes a value even when the percentage band does not. It exists because 20% of a side salad's fat is half a teaspoon of oil.

The one rule that governs all of this: a figure written in prose is a snapshot of the day it was written. Every number here was re-derived by running the scripts. If a document and a script disagree, the script is right.
