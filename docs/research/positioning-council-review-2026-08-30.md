# Positioning council review — `.agents/product-marketing.md` v1

> **Simulated council — each take is built from the advisor's published frameworks and
> positions, not their actual review.** No advisor named here has seen Menu Scan, and none
> has an opinion about it. Direct quotation is used only where the line is verifiable in the
> cited source; everything else is paraphrase of a documented position applied to this case.
> Run with the `marketing-council` skill, 2026-08-30. Live research pass: yes (see Sources).

---

## The question before the council

**"Is this the right positioning for Menu Scan, and where is it weakest?"**

Menu Scan is a pre-launch consumer mobile app: photograph a restaurant's paper menu, get every
dish re-ranked against your nutritional goals. `.agents/product-marketing.md` v1 bets on three
things simultaneously — **scan-first** (not a restaurant database), **goal-ranked output** (not a
calorie number), and **bilingual English/Spanish** — lists in **Health & Fitness**, and adopts
**"honesty, not precision"** as a binding accuracy stance. It positions against MenuFit
(54k ratings, database lookup, traction from a creator-video engine rather than the product).

**Stakes.** This document is about to harden into the input every downstream marketing skill
reads. The category is winner-take-most (top 5% of new apps earn ~400× the bottom 25%), the
proven channel is creator-led short-form video, and MyFitnessPal now owns Cal AI. A wrong
position here is not a copy problem, it is eighteen months.

### April Dunford — positioning as a chosen competitive frame

Her first question is not about the wedge. It is *"if Menu Scan didn't exist, what would this
person honestly do instead?"* — and the document answers it well in one place and badly in
another. The Competitive Landscape section correctly names the status quo (*"asking the server,
googling the restaurant, or not caring… and it is free"*) as the honest competitor. The
Objections table then answers MenuFit, Menu Order AI, MyFitnessPal and the menu itself — and
never answers the server. In *Sales Pitch* her position is that the pitch must beat **indecision**,
not just named rivals; the status quo is the alternative that wins most deals. A five-row
objection table with no row for *"I'll just order the salad and ask for dressing on the side"* is
a positioning document that has scoped its competition to the apps it researched.

On the three-part wedge: her chain runs alternatives → unique attributes → **value themes** → who
cares → market category. Attributes are *supposed* to group. Three attributes are not three
positions if they roll up into one value; in *The No Differentiation Illusion* she writes that
*"Delivering a combination of value counts, even when individual parts are not 'unique.'"*
Scan-first, goal-ranked and bilingual do roll up cleanly into one theme — **it produces a usable
answer at the table, wherever you are and whatever language the menu is in** — and each leg is
load-bearing for that theme. So the wedge survives her test, but only barely, and not in the form
written. Her operative rule, from the multiple-segments guide, is that *"The simpler we can keep
our positioning, the stronger it will be and the easier it will be for the team to make it real
in the market."* The doc currently presents three co-equal bets plus a six-row differentiator
table. That is an attribute inventory presented at the altitude of a position.

The genuine break is one level down, in the segment table. Her documented view is that *"multiple
target segments rarely need distinct positioning"* — **because the differentiated value is the
same across them**. Here it is not. For the US goal-tracker the competitive alternative is MenuFit
and the value is *"the database doesn't have your independent."* For the Spanish-speaking diner
the competitive alternative is **nothing** and the value is *"a thing exists at all."* Different
alternatives, different value, different proof. By her own test that is the signal that you are
looking at two positionings — or one product that must choose which of them it is playing. The
document never chooses, and never names which of her three positioning styles it is using.
Read literally it is playing **head-to-head against MenuFit in English**, the hardest style, on
the most contested square of its own positioning map, with zero distribution. The Spanish market
is the **big fish, small pond** play sitting unclaimed in the same document, listed third in the
differentiator table and second in the segment table.

**Bottom line:** the wedge is one position, but the doc is written as three; and the segment table
quietly contains two positionings, one of which is far easier to win and is not the one the
document leads with.

### Eugene Schwartz — market sophistication and the mechanism the doc forbids itself

His diagnostic is not "is honesty good." It is *"how many times has this market already heard
this claim?"* The answer is in the research: Cal AI's founder markets "90% accurate," MenuFit
claims "every restaurant in the world," and users respond by **computing the error themselves and
cancelling** (28% at Tazikis, 40% at McDonald's, quoted verbatim in the teardown). That is a
market at **sophistication stage three or four** — claims are exhausted and disbelieved. The
document reads that evidence correctly and then draws the wrong prescription from it.

In *Breakthrough Advertising* the answer at stage three is not to stop claiming. It is to
**introduce a new mechanism** — and at stage four, to elaborate that mechanism until the "how it
works" becomes the headline. Honesty is not a mechanism. It is the absence of a claim, and an
absence cannot carry a headline. Worse, the document owns a real mechanism and explicitly
forbids using it: *"the model supplies knowledge and our code does the arithmetic… it never
appears in copy."* That sentence is the single most correctable error in the document. A
mechanism is not an accuracy percentage. Describing *how the estimate is produced* makes no
numerical claim and is precisely what a jaded market will still listen to. So is the Chen et al.
finding, restated as a user-facing mechanism rather than a statistic: *tell it what's in the
dish and the estimate sharpens.* Keep the ban on the number. Lift the ban on the mechanism.
They are not the same ban.

Second correction, on awareness. His rule is that the prospect's stage dictates where the ad
starts. At the table, this prospect is **problem-aware and solution-unaware** — she knows the
menu has no calories; she does not know that photographing a menu is a thing an app does. The
document's one-liner ("see every dish re-ordered by your goals") is written for a
**product-aware** reader: it assumes she already accepts that menu-scanning works and is now
choosing between vendors. That is the wrong entry point for the largest available audience.
Lead with the desire and the problem; the mechanic is the second sentence, not the first.

Third, and this is the objection to the Spanish bet, not to the accuracy stance: *"Copy cannot
create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already
exist… and focus those already existing desires onto a particular product."* The research
establishes that the **problem** is larger in Spanish-speaking markets — calorie labelling is not
mandatory in Spain, menus essentially never print calories. It establishes nothing about whether
the **desire** is larger, or whether it exists at scale. The document infers demand from problem
size. Those are different quantities, and the only Spanish-language customer voice anywhere in
the evidence base is a single billing complaint on the Mexican App Store. Every Spanish-language
line in the Customer Language section is, in fact, in English.

**Bottom line:** the accuracy stance is right about the number and wrong about the mechanism;
and the Spanish bet currently rests on problem size standing in for mass desire, which is the
one substitution his method forbids.

### Gary Vaynerchuk — is it showable, not is it sayable

Wrong test. The document asks whether the positioning is *"sayable to camera in one line"* and
then writes a 26-word one-liner with two subordinate clauses and an em-dash. His position in
*Day Trading Attention* is that **the creative is the variable, not the targeting** — you do not
need one line, you need a format that generates hundreds of platform-native pieces, and the
VaynerMedia summary of the book is blunt that the opening seconds decide everything, citing a
Reel that went from 5k to 175k views on a first-three-seconds change alone. A positioning line
is not a hook. Nobody stops scrolling for a value proposition.

So look at what MenuFit actually built, because the research already answered this: a **viral
fast-food-swap short-form engine** run by the founder. That is not a line, it is a **repeatable
format** — infinite restaurants, infinite dishes, one template. That is why it has 4,160
ratings/month and Menu Order AI, with better copy, has seven. Menu Order AI does not have a
positioning problem. It has a zero-content problem, and *"Marketers ruin everything"* (Inc.,
2015) is the counterpart warning: MenuFit's format is now being copied, so its price is rising.

Menu Scan's format advantage is that the product is *visually* better content than the
incumbent's. A screen recording of a real menu physically reordering itself is motion; a database
lookup is a list. And the bilingual leg is not a third feature here, it is a **hook generator**:
"can it read *this*?" over a handwritten Spanish menu is a stronger first second than any
nutrition claim, it works for an English-speaking audience who will never use the Spanish feature,
and Spanish-language short-form is materially underpriced relative to English fitness content —
which is the whole thesis of the book. That is a channel argument for the Spanish leg that is
independent of the positioning argument for it.

The tension the document should own: **the most differentiated thing is the least filmable and
the most filmable thing is the least differentiated.** Goal-ranking is the only thing nobody else
has, and it is abstract — a sorting rule. Scanning is the thing three tiny competitors also do,
and it is the shot. Resolve that in the creative brief, not by pretending the one-liner solves it.

**Bottom line:** replace "sayable in one line" with "showable in a repeatable format," name the
format, and treat the Spanish menu as a hook asset for the English audience as well as a feature
for the Spanish one.

### Rory Sutherland — the disclaimer is not the reframe, and rank survives what numbers don't

The honesty stance is directionally right for a reason the document does not state. His reading
of costly signalling is that a signal persuades in proportion to what it costs the sender —
declining the easy "90% accurate" claim your competitor makes is expensive, and costliness
carries meaning. And in *Alchemy* he argues that powerful messages contain *"an element of
absurdity, illogicality, costliness... or extravagance."* Telling people your numbers might be
wrong is all four. So: yes, candour can be a differentiator.

But not as written. The document's honesty is **hedging**, not reframing — *"AI-estimated.
Confirm allergens with restaurant staff before ordering"* is a legal disclaimer wearing a brand
voice. The reframes he routinely cites work because they contain a **"so"**: Avis's "We're number
two, so we try harder"; Guinness turning a slow pour into "good things come to those who wait."
Avis did not say "we are number two (disclaimer)." Menu Scan has no *so*. Supply one and the
stance becomes sellable; leave it and it is a warning label that the category leader's 90% claim
will out-shout every time. The counterintuitive version a committee would reject, and therefore
the one worth testing: show a **range** rather than a point estimate, visibly, where competitors
show a confident number. It looks worse. That is what makes it a signal.

The deeper problem is a **doorman fallacy**. The document has accepted its competitor's framing —
that the product's job is to produce a calorie number — and then spends its accuracy rule
apologising for that number's imprecision. But the product does not sell a number. It sells an
**order**. And an ordering is structurally far more robust to estimation error than an absolute
value is: a ~30% error on both sides rarely flips two dishes that differ by a factor of two, and
the user's actual decision is "which of these three," not "how many calories exactly." That
argument is available today, it is honest, it is a reason to *believe* rather than a reason to
forgive — and it appears nowhere in the document. Defining the value as the number and then
regretting it destroys the unmeasured value the ranking already has.

One more reframe the document is leaving on the table. It treats "we can't tell you how accurate
we are" as the weak spot. Psycho-logically the weak spot is elsewhere: the user's fear is not
*"is this 12% off"* — it is *"will it read this menu at all, and will I look stupid holding my
phone over the table."* Solve the feeling, not the arithmetic.

**Bottom line:** honesty is a real asset but only with a "so" attached; and the strongest honest
claim available — that a *ranking* survives the error that kills a *number* — is missing entirely.

### Byron Sharp — the designated dissenter

His objection is to the premise. Menu Order AI has the sharpest copy in the market, a defined
niche, free unlimited scans, and 69 ratings in nine months. The document reads that as *"proof
that positioning alone does not buy traction"* and then produces four thousand more words of
positioning and one paragraph on channel, ending in *"see `influencer-marketing`."* The ratio is
inverted relative to the document's own finding. Menu Order AI does not lack differentiation. It
lacks **mental and physical availability**, and double jeopardy predicts exactly what happened:
a brand with almost no buyers also gets slightly less loyalty from the few it has. Differentiation
buyers cannot perceive, because they have never encountered the brand at all, is not a strategy.
Be fair about the limit, since critics of his own framework press it hardest here: *How Brands
Grow* is built on FMCG panel data and offers thin guidance to a brand starting from zero
availability. That is a genuine caveat, and it does not rescue a document with no availability
plan in it.

Two concrete, checkable failures. First: **the document has no distinctive assets section at
all.** No colour, no consistent name treatment, no sound, no recurring visual device. In a
category whose proven channel is short-form video — where a viewer sees you for two seconds
between other people's clips — the absence of a distinctive asset costs more than any nuance in
the wedge. The re-sorting animation is a candidate asset: codify it, use it identically every
time, never redesign it. His standing warning is that heavy creative rotation weakens memory
structures, which is exactly the failure mode of a volume-video strategy without a fixed asset.

Second: **the category-choice ruling is causally unsupported and the document states it as
settled.** It says Forq *"ships the closest feature list in the market and sits in Food & Drink,
where nobody with nutrition intent is searching, and has 1 rating in 3 months. Same product,
wrong shelf."* That is n = 1, confounded with everything. Forq is two months old with zero
distribution. Inside Health & Fitness — the shelf the document is endorsing — the same evidence
shows FoodieFit at ~181 ratings/month and Menu Order AI at ~7. A 25× spread on the *same* shelf
means the shelf is not the explanatory variable. This is the document's own standing discipline
about auto-generated directory blurbs, applied inconsistently: it demands a primary source before
believing a competitor's mechanic, then rules on category placement from a single confounded data
point. Ask instead *"is this insight just double jeopardy in disguise?"* — because it is.

The right lens is **category entry points**, and it reframes the question. The CEP is *"I have
just sat down and I don't know what to order."* Nobody browses the App Store at the table. So
the store category is not a positioning decision at all; it is a **physical availability**
(findability) decision, driven by what people type into search, and it should be settled by
search-term data and treated as reversible — not written into a positioning document as a
ruling. Note also that the CEP does not require the "nutrition app" frame to win: "menu in a
language I can't read" and "forty dishes and no pictures" are entry points into the same moment
from a different need, and Forq's visual-menu play is aimed at exactly those. The document's
segment table lists four segments and its anti-persona list then excludes *"people who rarely eat
out."* Tight targeting caps growth. Someone who eats out twice a month is the modal category
buyer, not an anti-persona.

**Bottom line:** the positioning is fine and it is not the binding constraint; the document has no
distinctive assets, no availability plan, and one causal claim (the shelf) that its own evidence
contradicts.

## Where the council disagrees

**1. Is the three-part wedge one position or three? — Dunford vs. Sharp**
Dunford says it resolves into one value theme and the fix is editorial: state the theme, demote
the legs to supporting attributes, name the positioning style. Sharp says the question is
close to irrelevant — buyers will not perceive a three-legged differentiator or a one-legged one,
and the effort belongs in distinctive assets and reach. **The real trade-off:** whether this
category's purchase is *considered* (comparison shopping in the App Store, where a sharp frame
wins) or *low-involvement* (a two-second video, where recognition wins). **What would settle it:**
how people actually arrive — search-term data and store-listing conversion vs. video-referred
installs. If most installs are video-referred, Sharp is right and the wedge wording barely
matters; if most are search-driven, Dunford's frame is the whole game. Nobody has this data yet
and the document should say so rather than assume the first.

**2. Which leg leads? — Dunford + Vaynerchuk (Spanish) vs. Schwartz (not yet)**
Dunford's segment analysis and Vaynerchuk's channel economics converge independently on the
Spanish leg: it is the only uncontested position (big fish, small pond), the only one the
incumbent cannot ship in a sprint, and it sits in underpriced attention. Schwartz dissents on the
one thing that matters — the evidence establishes a larger *problem* in Spanish-speaking markets
and says nothing about *desire*, willingness to pay, or category habit there. **The real
trade-off:** defensibility vs. demonstrated demand. **What would settle it:** Spanish-language
voice-of-customer — the exact gap the document already logs as open (`customer-research` Mode 2,
step 1.7). Until that runs, "bilingual" is the most defensible leg and the least evidenced one,
and those two facts should be stated together.

**3. Is honesty sellable? — Sutherland vs. Schwartz**
Sutherland says yes, as costly signalling, but only with a "so" attached and only if it is
reframed rather than disclaimed. Schwartz says honesty is not a mechanism and cannot carry a
headline in a stage-four market; what carries it is the *how*, which the document has and has
banned from copy. **The real trade-off:** whether candour is a *claim* or merely the *absence*
of one. They are reconcilable and the reconciliation is the recommendation: keep the ban on the
number, lift the ban on the mechanism, attach a "so" to the honesty. **What would settle it:**
a straight A/B — mechanism-led store copy vs. candour-led — which is the one thing neither of
them, nor this council, can settle by argument. (Hopkins, not seated, would have said only this.)

**4. Does the store category matter? — Dunford vs. Sharp**
Dunford's market-category component says the frame tells the buyer what to compare you to, and
Health & Fitness invites comparison with MyFitnessPal on features Menu Scan does not have — note
the document then spends five table rows denying it is a logger, which is the smell of a frame
fighting its own product. Sharp says the App Store shelf is physical availability, not mental,
the CEP happens at the table where no store is open, and the document's evidence for the ruling
(Forq, n = 1) is confounded. **The real trade-off:** category as *interpretive frame* vs. category
as *findability channel*. **What would settle it:** App Store search-volume and competition data
for the actual query set — a `aso` question with a real answer, not a positioning judgment.

**5. What does Menu Order AI's failure predict? — Sharp vs. Dunford**
Sharp: it predicts that Menu Scan, shipping with better positioning and the same zero
availability, gets the same 69 ratings; positioning was never the operative variable. Dunford's
defence is narrower than it looks: Menu Order AI has **sharp copy**, which is messaging, and
messaging is downstream of positioning — it has no stated market category beyond "GLP-1," which
is a segment, and its own listing positions by negation (*"No logging. No searching a database"*),
which tells a buyer what it isn't. That is a real distinction and it is also a small one.
**The real trade-off:** how much of the 20–500× traction gap is attributable to anything the
product or its words did. **What would settle it:** it is already settled as far as the evidence
goes — the research attributes MenuFit's lead to a founder-run video engine and Cal AI's to
150–250 influencers on retainer. Both councils' answers point at the same place, and it is not
this document.

**Where the council converges** (stated because a converging council is worth naming): all five
endorse the ban on marketing an accuracy percentage. All five treat the status quo — asking the
server — as the real competitor. None of them believes the document's evidence base is weak;
the disagreement is entirely about what to do with it.

## Chair's synthesis

**The verdict: the positioning is broadly right and the document is over-weighted toward
positioning at the expense of the two things that decide this category — a repeatable content
format and a distinctive asset. The wedge survives, but it is written at the wrong altitude, its
three legs are listed in ascending order of defensibility, and one of its rulings (the shelf) is
stated with more confidence than its evidence carries.**

The ordering point deserves its own line, because it is the single most actionable finding.
Rank the legs by how hard they are for MyFitnessPal-owned Cal AI to copy once it decides to
ship menu scanning — which the document names as its own clock:

| Leg | Copyable by the incumbent in | Contested by |
|---|---|---|
| Scan-first | a prompt change (Cal AI already takes photos) | 3 tiny apps, and soon the giant |
| Goal-ranked output | a sprint | nobody today |
| Bilingual, menu's-own-words | a data, eval and QA program | nobody, and structurally hardest |

The document lists these 1, 2, 3 — in the exact reverse of their defensibility, and calls the
Spanish diner segment #2. Lead with the hardest-to-copy leg as the **beachhead**, keep scan-first
as the **mechanism** and goal-ranking as the **output form**. That is one position with three
roles rather than three bets, and it is the change that makes Dunford's simplicity rule and
Vaynerchuk's channel economics point the same way.

Caveat, held openly: Schwartz's objection to that ordering is unresolved. Nothing in the evidence
base shows Spanish-language *desire* at scale, only Spanish-language *problem*. Do not harden the
beachhead until step 1.7 runs.

### Do

1. **Rewrite the wedge as one value theme, and name the positioning style.** One sentence of
   differentiated value ("a usable answer at the table, wherever you are, in whatever language
   the menu is printed in"), with scan-first / goal-ranked / bilingual demoted to the attributes
   that produce it. State explicitly: **big fish, small pond** on independents-and-Spanish — not
   head-to-head with MenuFit, which is what the document currently reads as.
2. **Split the accuracy rule in two.** Keep 🔴 *do not market an accuracy percentage* exactly as
   written. Delete *"it never appears in copy"* from the differentiation section and add a
   permitted-mechanism clause: how the estimate is produced, and that one line about ingredients
   sharpens it, are mechanism statements, not numeric claims. Add a "so" to the honesty stance
   (Sutherland) so it reframes rather than disclaims.
3. **Add the rank-robustness argument to the Objections table.** *"How do I know the calories are
   right?"* currently answers with candour alone. Add the structural point: the product returns an
   **order**, and an order survives estimation error that would destroy a number. This is honest,
   available pre-launch, invents no metric, and is the only answer in the set that is a reason to
   buy rather than a reason to forgive.
4. **Demote the category ruling from decision to hypothesis.** The Forq evidence is n = 1 and
   confounded; the same shelf holds a 25× traction spread. Rewrite as: *Health & Fitness is the
   working assumption, to be settled by store search data* — and hand it to `aso`, not to
   positioning.
5. **Add a Distinctive Assets section — the document has none.** Nominate the re-sort motion as
   the primary asset; commit to identical use and no rotation. This is the largest single
   omission relative to the category's proven channel.
6. **Replace "sayable to camera in one line" with "showable in a repeatable format,"** and name
   the format in the channel note. The incumbent's engine is a template (one swap, one
   restaurant, infinitely repeatable), not a line. Flag the tension for the creative brief:
   the differentiated leg (ranking) is abstract, the filmable leg (scanning) is contested.
7. **Two smaller corrections.** Add a status-quo row to Objections ("I'll just ask the server")
   — Dunford's point that the pitch must beat indecision. And drop *"people who rarely eat out"*
   from the anti-personas: light buyers are where category growth comes from, and twice a month
   is the modal diner, not an edge case.

### Tripwire

**Sharp's.** The signal to watch is the ratio of effort: if, ninety days from launch, this
document has been revised more times than a distinctive asset has been shipped or a content
format has been run, the council's majority reading is that Menu Scan is repeating Menu Order AI
with better prose. The countable version: **pieces of platform-native video published per week**,
against **positioning-document revisions per week**. If the second number is ever the larger one,
stop editing.

Secondary tripwire, **Schwartz's**: if step 1.7 comes back with no Spanish-language voice-of-
customer — no forum threads, no reviews, no expressed desire — then the beachhead recommendation
above is unsupported and the English independent-restaurant segment leads instead.
